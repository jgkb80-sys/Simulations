3D simulation of three flasks reacting simultaneously: calcium carbonate as large lumps, small chips, and powder, each with hydrochloric acid on its own digital balance, where powder fizzes fastest and stops bubbling first, lumps fizz slowly and keep going far longer, all three mass readings are plotted live on one shared graph, the simulation keeps running until every flask — including the slow-reacting lumps — has fully consumed its calcium carbonate, and bubbles in each flask stop forming once that flask's reaction is finished but drift upward and fade away rather than freezing. A plain yellow label above each flask names its surface-area condition. Sliders let the view be scrolled up, down, left, or right in addition to the existing rotate and zoom controls. An optional close-up view shows three small circular panels, one floating above each flask, comparing how often red HCl particles collide with the exposed marble surface: a single large square for the lump, several medium squares for the chips, and many small squares for the powder, with both the exposed surface and the number of colliding particles increasing together so collisions visibly happen far more often in the powder panel than in the lump panel.

Three curves all fall from 150 grams toward about 149.56 grams as carbon dioxide escapes. Powder plateaus within seconds, small chips plateau within about two minutes, and large lumps take much longer but the simulation keeps running until they finish too.

All three flasks start with the same mass of marble and excess acid, so they all lose the same total mass in the end (about 0.44 g of CO₂) — surface area only changes how fast that mass is lost. The simulation keeps running until even the large lumps are fully consumed. Watch the bubbling stop in each flask as its curve flattens out — and notice how much more vigorously the powder fizzes than the lumps, since it has far more surface area exposed to the acid. The "Show collision close-up" button illustrates why: the same red HCl particles collide with the marble far more often as it's broken into smaller pieces, since more of its surface is exposed at once — count the green flashes in each panel and you'll see collisions happen fastest in the powder panel and slowest in the lump panel.
