3D simulation of two flasks of hydrogen peroxide solution, one with no catalyst and one with manganese dioxide catalyst added, each flask connected by a tube to its own gas syringe that collects the oxygen gas produced as the peroxide decomposes. Both flasks start with the same concentration and volume of hydrogen peroxide at the same temperature, so the presence of the catalyst is the only variable — both eventually produce the same total volume of oxygen, but the catalyzed flask reaches that volume dramatically faster while the uncatalyzed flask barely reacts at all within the same time. A toggle lets the catalyst be added as coarse granules or as a fine powder, showing that increasing the catalyst's surface area speeds the reaction up further still.

The catalyzed flask's curve shoots up almost immediately and flattens out near the top of the graph within about a minute. The uncatalyzed flask's curve stays almost flat along the bottom for the whole graph, since without a catalyst the reaction is far too slow to produce a noticeable volume of gas in this time.

Both flasks start with the same concentration and volume of hydrogen peroxide at the same temperature — the only difference is whether MnO₂ catalyst has been added. Because the amount of H₂O₂ is identical in both, they would eventually release the same total volume of oxygen — but without a catalyst that takes an extremely long time, so the uncatalyzed curve barely moves within this simulation. Switching the catalyst between granules and powder changes how much surface area is exposed to the peroxide: more surface area means more frequent effective collisions and a faster rate, even though the catalyst's activation energy is exactly the same either way — MnO₂ opens up a route with a much lower activation energy, which is why far more collisions succeed at the same temperature, while switching between granules and powder does not change that; surface area changes the rate, not the activation energy.
