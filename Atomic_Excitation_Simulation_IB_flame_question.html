S1.3 · Atomic energy and light

Excitation & photon emission

Energy raises an electron to an excited state. Returning to a lower state releases a photon whose energy matches the gap. At the ionization limit (n = ∞), the electron leaves the atom and no return photon is shown.

The flame-test laboratory

Light the burner, choose a metal salt, then introduce it on a clean wire loop. Observe the characteristic flame colour.

Burner off

Light the burner to begin.

Heat supplies energy to species derived from the salt. As excited electrons move to lower energy levels, light is emitted; the combined visible emissions give the characteristic flame colour.

Flame colours are visual approximations based on Royal Society of Chemistry flame-test observations. Copper chloride can produce blue-green light; potassium’s lilac is often faint. Sodium contamination can mask other colours. Flame shape, brightness and persistence are illustrative, and displayed colours depend on the screen.

Model, accuracy and sources

Rings represent energy levels schematically, not actual circular electron orbits. Their radii, dashed attraction lines, wave spacing and animation times are illustrative. Larger energy gaps produce higher frequency and shorter wavelength, using ΔE = hf = hc/λ. Wave spacing uses a compressed scale so UV and IR can both be seen.

Hydrogen uses the approximate energy-level model Eₙ = −13.5984346/n² eV; fine structure is omitted. Continuous mode chooses random n = 2–7 and illustrative decay paths, not measured transition probabilities. Only the changing electron is animated; individual angular-momentum substates are not resolved.

Neutral helium contains two electrons. The comparison uses its measured 1s² ¹S₀ → 1s4p ¹P°₁ energy gap (191492.7093035 cm⁻¹ = 23.742070 eV), not a hydrogen-like Z²/n² model. The labels n = 1 and n = 4 refer to the ground configuration and the promoted electron’s principal level. Electron shielding and electron–electron interactions affect helium’s energies. Both atoms receive their own required excitation energy and return directly to ground in this comparison; it does not claim every helium transition has a larger gap than every hydrogen transition.

Heat and electrical excitation transfer energy through sufficiently energetic collisions. A photon must match an allowed energy gap to be absorbed. Ordinary classroom heating is insufficient to produce the large atomic excitations shown; this model assumes collisions energetic enough to excite the atom. Real excited-state lifetimes are vastly shorter than the animation.

Sources: NIST neutral-helium energy levels; NIST hydrogen data; NIST photon-energy relation; NIST collision excitation.
