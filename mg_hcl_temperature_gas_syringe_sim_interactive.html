3D simulation of two flasks of magnesium metal reacting with hydrochloric acid, both heated on identical-looking digital hot plates but set to two different temperatures, each flask connected by a tube to its own gas syringe that collects the hydrogen gas produced. Both flasks start with the same amount of magnesium and the same volume and concentration of hydrochloric acid, so temperature is the only variable — both eventually produce the same total volume of hydrogen, but the higher-temperature flask reaches that volume far faster, so its live graph line rises much more steeply before both level off at the same height. An optional close-up view shows two circular panels, one above each flask, comparing how much faster the HCl particles move and strike the magnesium surface at the higher temperature, illustrating greater average kinetic energy.

Both curves level off at the same final volume since the amount of reactants is identical in each flask — temperature only changes how fast that volume is reached. The high-heat curve is much steeper and finishes first; the low-heat curve is gentler and takes longer to reach the same height.

Both flasks start with the same mass of magnesium and the same volume and concentration of hydrochloric acid — the only thing that differs is temperature, controlled by heating one flask's hot plate to a low setting and the other to a high setting. Because the amount of each reactant is identical, both flasks eventually release the same total volume of hydrogen — the two graph lines level off at the same height. What changes is how quickly they get there: the high-heat flask's particles carry more kinetic energy, so collisions between HCl and the magnesium surface are both more frequent and more often energetic enough to react, producing gas much faster right from the start.
