3D simulation of three flasks of calcium carbonate marble chips reacting with hydrochloric acid of three different concentrations — dilute, medium, and concentrated — each flask connected by a tube to its own gas syringe that collects the carbon dioxide produced. Hydrochloric acid is the limiting reagent and marble is in excess and identical in every flask, so the more concentrated acid both reacts faster and produces a larger final volume of gas; the three live graph lines rise at different rates and level off at three different heights rather than converging, and the colour of each solution fades toward the same pale shade as its acid is used up. An optional close-up view shows three small circular panels, one floating above each flask, comparing how much more frequently acid particles collide with the marble surface as concentration increases.

Three curves all rise from zero and level off, but at three different heights: dilute levels off near 12 cubic centimeters, medium near 24, and concentrated near 48, with the concentrated curve also rising the fastest at the start.

Every flask starts with the same excess of marble chips (so surface area is not a variable here) and the same volume of acid — only the acid's concentration differs. Because hydrochloric acid is the limiting reagent in every flask, a more concentrated acid contains more moles of HCl in that same volume, so it produces a genuinely larger final volume of CO₂ — the three curves level off at three different heights and never meet. As the acid is used up, the colour of every solution fades toward the same pale shade, since all three end up as the same dilute calcium chloride solution once the limiting HCl runs out. The steeper initial slope for the more concentrated acid reflects collision theory: more HCl particles packed into the same volume collide with the marble surface more often, so gas is produced faster right from the start.
